## Definition

A bundle uses demand that already exists to move something that has none. The
fast product supplies the traffic; the slow product supplies the margin
opportunity. Done well, the customer feels helped rather than sold to.

## Industry best practices

- **Anchor on a proven mover.** The bundle only works if one item was going to
  sell anyway.
- **Pair within an occasion, not within a category.** "Taco night" beats "two
  tequilas".
- **Three items maximum.** Beyond three it is a shopping list, and the customer
  edits it.
- **Price the bundle as one thing.** "$34 for the kit" converts better than
  three items with a percentage off, because it removes the arithmetic.
- **Physically co-locate.** A bundle that requires walking to three aisles is a
  suggestion, not a bundle.

## Common mistakes

- **Bundling two slow products.** Zero plus zero.
- **Discounting the anchor.** You have given away margin on the item that needed
  no help.
- **Unnatural pairings.** Customers notice when the logic is "we need to move
  this", and it costs trust.

## Recommended actions

1. Take your **fastest mover in a category** and your **largest frozen position
   in the same category**.
2. Check the pairing makes sense to a shopper, not just to a spreadsheet.
3. Build it physically, price it as one item, sign it with the reason.
4. Two-week window, then reassess.

## Benchmarks

- Attach rate on a natural pairing: **10–15%**
- Three items maximum
- Bundle windows: **2–4 weeks**

## Store tips

The most reliable bundle in a liquor store is the **cocktail kit**: spirit,
mixer, garnish. It solves a real problem — the customer knows what they want to
drink but not what to buy to make it — and it moves mixers, which are usually
the slowest, least considered stock in the shop.

## AI notes

Only bundle within a category or a genuine occasion. Never anchor on a product
that is out of stock. The attach rate is an industry assumption with no basis in
this store's data — the POS export has no basket-level lines, so say so.
