## Definition

Inventory health is not "do I have enough". It is whether the money sitting on
your shelves is working. Every bottle is cash you already spent, converted into
something you hope a customer wants. A healthy shop converts it back quickly and
repeatedly; an unhealthy one has quietly turned its bank balance into glass.

The single number that matters is **weeks of supply**: stock on hand divided by
weekly sales rate. It answers "if I stopped buying today, how long until this
runs out?" — and it is the only inventory figure that means the same thing for a
$9 vodka and a $200 scotch.

## Industry best practices

- Target **6–8 weeks of cover** on core lines. Enough to absorb a delivery
  delay, not enough to fund a warehouse.
- Review the **top 20 positions by cash tied up** monthly, not the whole range.
  A 1,400-SKU review never happens twice; a 20-line review happens every month.
- **Buy to a sales rate, not to a case size.** The distributor's case is 12
  because that is how it ships, not because you sell 12.
- Treat **negative stock counts as urgent data repair**, not as a rounding
  error. Every downstream number inherits the error.
- Kill a SKU when it has had **two full seasons with no movement**. Waiting for
  the customer who wanted it is how a range becomes a museum.

## Common mistakes

- **Confusing a full shelf with a healthy shelf.** A wall of product looks like
  a well-run shop and is often the opposite.
- **Reordering on sight.** "I noticed we were low" is not a signal; it is a
  glance at a facing that might hold four months of cover.
- **Deep-buying a deal on a slow mover.** The saving is real and the cash is
  gone for a year.
- **Never taking a loss.** A bottle marked down 40% today is better than the
  same bottle at full price in eighteen months. The money you spent is already
  spent; the only question is when you get it back.
- **Treating all slow stock the same.** Something with 30 weeks of cover needs a
  buying change. Something with 200 weeks needs a clearance.

## Recommended actions

1. **Today** — fix negative counts, and reorder anything sold out that was
   selling. Out-of-stock is the only inventory problem that costs money every
   single day and can never be recovered later.
2. **This week** — pull the ten largest frozen positions and decide on each:
   discount, bundle, or return to supplier.
3. **This month** — cut reorder quantities on everything above 12 weeks. The
   cheapest clearance is the order you don't place.
4. **This quarter** — run a phased markdown on the sleeping tail, worst first.

## Seasonality

Cover requirements are not constant. Beer and RTD need tight cover — they are
perishable and dated. Spirits tolerate depth. Wine sits between: a Cabernet will
keep, but a rosé bought in August is a next-June problem. Build stock in the six
weeks before a gifting season and stop buying two weeks before it, or January
opens with a wall of Christmas packaging.

## KPIs

- Weeks of supply, per product and per category
- Inventory turnover (annualised)
- Share of inventory value in stock moving slower than 26 weeks
- Count of products sold out that had sales
- Negative stock count (a data-quality measure, not a business one)

## Benchmarks

- Turnover **4–6x/year** for a US independent
- **6–8 weeks** cover on core lines
- Under **20% of inventory value** in stock slower than 26 weeks
- **95%+** in-stock rate on the top 100 sellers

## Store tips

The fastest inventory win in any shop is the **sold-out fast mover**. It costs
nothing to fix, the demand is proven, and every day it stays empty is a customer
walking to whoever is open down the road. Fix that before touching the slow tail.

The second fastest is a **buying change, not a clearance**. Clearances recover
60 cents on the dollar. Not over-buying recovers 100.

## AI notes

Never recommend reordering something with more than 12 weeks of cover, however
well it once sold. Never recommend discounting something with under 4 weeks —
that is margin given away on sales already coming. Rank slow stock by cash tied
up, never by count: ten bottles of a $90 scotch matter more than eighty bottles
of a $6 mixer.
